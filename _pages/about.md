I am a PhD candidate in Political Economics at the Stanford Graduate School of Business. My job market paper asks whether the building blocks of democratic accountability can be found in a domain deliberately insulated from ordinary politics: antitrust enforcement. I study whether average voters understand mergers and acquisitions and can form opinions about them, whether those opinions influence their confidence in government agencies, and whether actual enforcement tracks public opinion.

**I am on the 2026–2027 academic job market.**

My research explores public opinion and political behavior at the intersection of economics, policy, and political institutions: whether average voters can judge and discipline technocratic government, how individuals' interactions with the economy and their labor-market experiences shape political attitudes and behavior, and how candidates' strategies and changing information environments reshape what information reaches the public in the first place. Grounded in formal modeling and the quantitative training of economics, my work combines survey experiments, administrative data, and computational social science methods to rigorously examine the mechanisms behind political behavior.
